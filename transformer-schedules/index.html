Depicting the schedule inside a transformer

We draw transformers as dataflow — boxes, arrows, attention heatmaps. But almost every question anyone actually has about a transformer in production is a question about time on contended hardware. That is a different object, and it needs a different picture.

Why

The picture we draw answers a question nobody is asking

The dominant transformer visual is the attention heatmap: token × token, colored by weight. It became dominant partly because it is genuinely illuminating and partly because it is cheap to render — a softmax output is already a matrix, and a matrix is already a heatmap. The encoding was handed to us by the data structure.

But look at what people actually ask once a model leaves the notebook. Why is throughput half of what the FLOP count predicts. Where did the GPU-seconds go. Why does p99 latency spike when a long prompt arrives. Why does this fit in memory at four-way pipeline depth but not eight. Why is the model 60% idle.

None of those are answerable from an attention map. All of them are scheduling questions.

A schedule is not a dataflow graph. A dataflow graph says what depends on what; a schedule says what ran where, when, and what sat idle while it did. The second has a shape the first cannot express: a contended resource extended over time. That shape has a canonical encoding — the resource × time timeline — and the whole design problem is what you must put on it before it starts answering questions.

What follows is an argument in three moves, each one a thing the naive timeline gets wrong. Every schedule below is computed live in the page by simulating the actual scheduling rule, not drawn by hand — so you can move the parameters and watch the claims hold.

Move 1 · the primitive

Draw the idle time, not the work

Pipeline parallelism splits a model across p devices and pushes m microbatches through. The classic picture shows the forward and backward passes marching across the devices. The instinct is to put the ink on the work — the work is what the model does.

That is backwards. In a schedule, the finding is almost always the emptiness. The bubble is where the money goes. So the idle region gets an explicit mark, and the total is stated in the same units you are billed in: device-seconds.

Move 2 · the punchline

Two schedules, identical timelines, and only one of them fits in memory

Switch the control above from GPipe to 1F1B and watch the readouts. The makespan does not change. The idle share does not change. On a pure timeline the two schedules are the same picture rearranged.

Yet 1F1B is what everyone actually runs. The reason is invisible on the timeline and visible in the fourth readout: GPipe holds every microbatch's activations until its backward pass arrives, so peak memory grows with m. 1F1B starts each backward as early as possible, so peak memory is bounded by p — the depth of the pipe, not the length of the batch. Push the microbatch slider right and watch GPipe's peak climb while 1F1B's stays flat.

A schedule visualization that shows only occupancy cannot explain why the dominant schedule in the field exists.

This is the general lesson, and it is why "just use a Gantt chart" is not an answer. Occupancy is one projection of a schedule. Residency — what is being held, not what is running — is an independent one, and for transformers it is usually the binding constraint. The picture needs both lanes.

Residency: activations held per device

The lane the timeline hides

Height is the number of microbatch activations resident on that device at that instant. Under GPipe the staircase climbs to m on every device before anything is released. Under 1F1B it plateaus at p−i and stays there — the sawtooth of a pipeline in steady state.

Move 3 · the second clock

Inference schedules break the axis you assumed

Training schedules are static: the shape is known before the run starts. Inference is not. Requests arrive when they arrive, prompts are of different lengths, and generation stops when the model emits a stop token — which nobody can predict.

Static batching waits for a full batch, runs it to completion, and returns. Because the batch cannot retire until its longest member finishes, every shorter request's slot sits occupied but useless, and every arriving request waits for the whole batch. Continuous batching schedules at the granularity of a single decode iteration: as soon as a sequence finishes, its slot takes a waiting request.

Here the two-clock problem bites. The x-axis of a training schedule is essentially logical — microbatch index and wall time agree. In inference they diverge, and the divergence is the latency. So the same encoding must carry per-request wall-clock spans and the slot occupancy underneath them.

What generalises

Five rules for depicting a schedule

  1. Resource × time, drawn to scale. Area is then device-seconds, which is money. The moment you stop drawing time linearly, the reader can no longer estimate cost from the picture, and a schedule diagram that cannot be measured is a diagram of a dataflow graph.
  2. Idle is a mark, not a background. The gap is the finding in nearly every real schedule. Give it an explicit encoding and a stated total; do not let it be the colour of the page.
  3. Occupancy and residency are different lanes. What is running and what is being held are independent, and for transformers the memory ceiling usually binds first. GPipe and 1F1B are indistinguishable without the second lane.
  4. A gap needs its cause attached. A timeline shows that a device stalled; only the dependency edge shows why. Link them — on hover, on selection, however — because the pair is the explanation and either alone is a bare fact.
  5. Say which clock. Logical order and wall-clock time coincide in training and diverge in serving. Their divergence is precisely what latency is, so the axis must be labelled and, where they differ, both must be visible.
On honesty. The schedules here are simulated from the scheduling rules under deliberately idealised assumptions: uniform stage time, no communication cost, no overlap of compute and transfer, and a backward pass costing the same as a forward. Real traces are messier, and the bubble in a real run is generally worse. These are the same idealisations the original pipeline-parallelism papers use to define the schedules, which is what makes them fair for comparing schedules — but they are not measurements, and nothing here should be read as a benchmark.
